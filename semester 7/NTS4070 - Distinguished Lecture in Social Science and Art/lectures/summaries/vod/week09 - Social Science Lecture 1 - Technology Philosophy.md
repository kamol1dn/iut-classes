## The essay and where it's going [00:06]

Heidegger's *The Question Concerning Technology* asks what technology is, what it does to us and how it relates to
the human being. It also shows his attitude to **modernity**, **metaphysics** and **being**. His target is
our **modern condition**, whether that's his own mid-20th century or "late modernity / post-modernity / late
capitalism", where his questions are "raised to a higher power". It's short and less dense than most of his work.

The road map [00:06–05:39]:

1. Our everyday ideas of technology are **correct but superficial**. They **conceal more than they reveal**.
   He calls them the **instrumental and anthropological** definitions [02:48].
2. He goes back to **Aristotle's four causes**. Aristotle was right about some things but didn't think them through
   as a phenomenologist should.
3. That leads to **truth as revealing (*aletheia*)**, to ***techne*** (the root of "technology") and to how techne
   ties in with ***episteme*** (science, disciplinary knowledge) [03:31].
4. The crux: is **modern** (mechanized, industrial) technology **radically different** from earlier technology [03:50]?
   Humans have always had technology: **flint knapping**, skinning animals, making tools that make tools.
   **Aristotle** already called the **hand "the tool of tools"**, called slaves **ensouled tools** and imagined
   **looms that weave by themselves** [04:23–04:40]. Heidegger's answer is **"yes and no"**.
5. The central feature of the modern attitude is the **standing-reserve, *Bestand*** [05:39].

## 1. Questioning and the "free relationship" [06:12]

- **"Questioning builds a way."** [06:18] Questioning is a **mode of thinking**, not a catechism that hunts for quick
  answers. You stay **patient and attentive** to the thing and don't pigeonhole it. His examples: questioning a person,
  a text, or a cup of coffee (where it came from, what the taste reveals) [07:01]. Questioning is part of Heidegger's
  **phenomenological / metaphysical method**.
- The goal is to **prepare a "free relationship" to technology** [07:27]. The relationship is free **if it opens our
  human existence to the essence of technology**. So **existence and essence** get linked.
- Humans are **by nature questioning beings** [08:29]. That's part of what makes us **Dasein**, Heidegger's term for
  the human kind of being [02:24].
- Seeing technology as a means is **correct but superficial**. You don't throw it away as a **"neo-Luddite"**
  [09:23]. But at the level of essence it **gets in the way**. It works as a **screen, a concealment**.
- **Metaphysics** here means revealing, stage by stage, **what the essences of things are**, with help from earlier
  metaphysicians (Plato, Aristotle, ... Nietzsche).

## 2. Technology is not the essence of technology [10:15]

**"Technology is not equivalent to the essence of technology."** Chalk, the press that molded it, his **glasses**,
the camera recording him [10:43–11:18] are all **instances** of technology. None of them is its essence.

- **The tree analogy** [11:31]: the essence of tree is **what pervades every tree as tree**, and it is not itself a
  tree among the other trees. So **the essence of technology is nothing technological**. It isn't one gadget or one
  "paradigm example".
- What he's steering towards (a spoiler): the essence lies largely in **human comportment** and in **how humans make
  meaning out of technology** [12:17].

### Three attitudes that all miss the essence [12:17–14:44]

| Attitude | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Passionately affirming it**: using, comparing, troubleshooting, obsessing over gadgets | stays inside "the technological" |
| **Putting up with it** / evading it, enduring it as a fact of life | also no access to the essence |
| **Rejecting it**: "technology is all bad, back to the garden" (hippie, Rousseauian, Emersonian tropes) [14:09] | same |
| **Treating it as neutral** | **the worst case.** "We are delivered over to it in the worst possible way when we regard it as something neutral" [14:44] |

**Against neutrality**: the common view says technology is **value-free**, a pure means for good or bad. Not true.
**"Every time that we introduce technology, we change our trajectory."**

## 3. The instrumental and anthropological definitions [15:44]

According to **ancient doctrine**, a thing's essence is **what it is**. There are two standard answers:

| Definition | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Instrumental** | technology is **a means to an end** |
| **Anthropological** | technology is **a human activity** |

You don't have to choose. **Both are right and they belong together**, because **positing ends and finding means is
the distinctively human activity**. Technology is an ***instrumentum*** (instrument, tool, aid) [17:16]. His
examples: chalkboard, camera, computer, office chair, electric light.

- The instrumental definition is **"uncannily correct"**. It holds for a mortar and pestle *and* for the iPhone or the
  banking system [17:38]. Heidegger's own list: the **power plant** with turbines and generators, the **jet aircraft**,
  high-frequency apparatus. A **radar station** is less simple than a **weather vane**, but it's still a means [18:04].
- So the instrumental conception **conditions every attempt to understand technology**. It's the **default
  starting point**. It's **correct but not adequate**.
- **The will to mastery** [19:10]: we want to "get technology intelligently in hand", to **master** it. That will
  gets **more urgent the more technology threatens to slip from human control**. His modern example: **screen-time
  apps** that tell you to get off your phone [19:30]. **We use technology to control technology.**
- The pivot: **what if technology were no mere means?** How would the will to master it stand then?

## 4. The correct vs the true [20:21]

- A correct statement **doesn't need to uncover the thing in its essence**. **"The merely correct is not yet the
  true"** [21:24]. The true comes out only where such an **uncovering** happens.
- Method: **"We must seek the true by way of the correct"** [21:41]. So ask: **what is the instrumental itself?**
  Where do means and ends belong?
- Whatever has an effect is a **cause**. A means is that by which something is effected. The **end** that decides the
  means is also a cause, the **final cause**. **"Wherever instrumentality reigns, there reigns causality"** [22:49].
  So the question moves from praxis ("a physics of the apparent") to **metaphysics**.

## 5. Aristotle's four causes: the silver chalice [23:50]

Heidegger uses the scholastic Latin names and the example of a **silver chalice** for a sacrificial rite [24:19]:

| Cause | Latin | Chalice | His pliers example [26:13] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | ***causa materialis*** | the **silver** | steel |
| Formal | ***causa formalis*** | the **chalice shape** | shape: gripping surface, teeth, nut, handle grip |
| Final | ***causa finalis*** | the **sacrificial rite** it's made for. It determines form *and* matter | to grip things better (technology for working with technology) |
| Efficient | ***causa efficiens*** | the **silversmith** | the factory, the die-press operator, the assembler |

Also a bakery version: flour, water and yeast, the loaf, the reason (money, appetite, bread for the king), the baker.

### Heidegger's questions about the doctrine [27:28]

- Suppose causality itself is **"veiled in darkness"**. We act as if the four causes **"fell from heaven"**. **Why
  only four?** What does *cause* really mean, and what unites them? [27:58]
- **Efficient cause took over.** Already in antiquity, and starkly in modern philosophy, the efficient cause became
  the **cause of causes**. **Descartes** and the Cartesians reduced everything to efficient (plus material and formal)
  causes. **Leibniz** tried to bring **final causes** back [28:49–28:56]. It goes so far that the **final cause
  (telic finality)** is **no longer counted as causality at all** [29:27]. Heidegger wants to make it *more* important.
- **Etymology** [29:55]: Latin ***causa*** / *casus* comes from ***cadere*, "to fall"** (how things turn out). German
  ***Ursache***, Greek ***aition*** [30:17] means **"that to which something is indebted."**

## 6. The four causes as four ways of being responsible [30:37]

All four causes are **ways of being responsible (indebted) for something**, and they **belong together**. It sounds
anthropomorphic, but:

- The chalice is **indebted to / owes thanks to the silver** for what it consists of. The silver is **co-responsible**
  [31:19].
- It is indebted to its **aspect, its *eidos*** (form) [31:45]. Why a chalice and not a **brooch or a ring**? Why an
  iPhone and not a pile of parts? Why a cup and not a cylinder with a hole in the bottom?
- **Above all** it is indebted to what **in advance confines it within the realm of consecration and bestowal**: the
  final cause [32:23]. It "circumscribes" the thing and gives it bounds. Final here doesn't mean last in time. It
  **precedes the whole making process**. It's the **meaning the thing has for humans**, so in a sense it's "more
  causal".
- **The silversmith is *not* a *causa efficiens*** [33:29–34:04]. He doesn't just produce the chalice as the
  "effect" of his making. **Aristotle had no such cause and no Greek word for it.** What the silversmith does is
  **consider carefully and gather together** the other three ways of being responsible [34:40].
  - "To consider carefully" is Greek ***legein*** (root of ***logos***). It's rooted in ***apophainesthai***:
    **to bring forward into appearance, to show** [35:00].
  - So the silversmith is co-responsible as **where the chalice's bringing-forth starts from**.
- Conclusion: **the four causes aren't on the same level**, and the maker doesn't fit the schema.

## 7. Occasioning and bringing-forth (*poiesis*) [36:44]

- **What unites the four?** The Greek sense of "being responsible" isn't moral fault ("owing" as a lapse) and it isn't
  "effecting". Both lose the primal meaning [36:44].
- Heidegger calls the four ways of being responsible **occasioning** (*Ver-an-lassen*), in a broad, metaphysical
  sense [38:16]. Here he ties the essay to his other works: ***Being and Time***, ***What Is Metaphysics?***, the essay
  on **the essence of truth**, and the one on **the work of art** [37:38].
- Occasioning **brings something into appearance**. That doesn't mean its surface. It means the thing **appears as a
  thing for human beings** who can give it meaning, use it, talk about it, question it, or even mistake it [38:46].
- The language isn't pushing (agency). It's **coaxing**: "come on, you want to exist, here's your chance."
- The four ways **"set it free to that place and so start it on its way, namely into its complete arrival"** [41:09].
  Making, whether art or everyday manufacture, **lets a thing become what lies latent in it as potential**. This
  **starting-on-its-way** is the main feature of being responsible.
- They **let what is not yet present arrive into presencing** [42:53].
- **Plato's *Symposium*** [43:02]: **"Every occasion for whatever passes beyond the non-present and goes forward into
  presencing is *poiesis*, bringing-forth."** [43:11]

### Two kinds of bringing-forth [43:39]

| | ***Physis*** (nature) | **Craft / art** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | **"upspringing"**: things swell up out of potential into existence | the weaver, shoemaker, poet, the politician who brings order |
| Example | a **seed bursting its husk** and seeking sustenance [44:01] | **flint** knapped into an **arrowhead** [45:39], the chalice |
| Where the bringing-forth lies | **in the thing itself** | **in another**: the craftsman or artist |

Bringing-forth isn't only handicraft or poetry. ***Physis* is bringing-forth too.** Things come to be in stages, either
by their own development or because someone takes them up.

## 8. Truth as unconcealment (*aletheia*) [46:10]

- **Bringing-forth goes from concealment into unconcealment** [46:26]. The chalice is "there" in the silver in a sense,
  but it takes the silversmith to make it so.
- Greek for this: ***aletheia***, which is **also the word for truth** [46:49]. **The truth of a thing is its
  unconcealment**: its coming into being.
- The **Romans translated it *veritas***. **We say "truth" and usually understand it as correctness of
  representation** [47:28]. Compare section 4: correct ≠ true.
- Unconcealment **isn't just visual or conceptual**. It means **being present, actual, meaningful "on the scene" for
  us humans**.
- **Bringing-forth gathers and rules** the four modes of occasioning [48:57]. It also covers **means and ends** and
  **instrumentality** itself. The maker, a **Dasein** who can set itself projects, gathers matter, form and end with
  thought, craft and time.

## 9. The result: technology is a mode of revealing [51:02]

> **"Technology is a mode of revealing"**: bringing into being, **introducing meaning** where there were only
> inchoate, chaotic or unperceived things. It doesn't create being out of nothing. It **organizes, structures, and
> gives life, sense and reality to things.**

The video ends here [51:46], at what he calls the "crescendo" of an early movement in the essay.

---

> **Not covered in this video (background, not the lecturer's words).** The rest of the essay argues that
> **modern** technology is also a revealing, but a **challenging-forth** (*Herausfordern*) rather than *poiesis*. It
> sets nature up as **standing-reserve (*Bestand*)**: resources on call to be stored and ordered. His examples are the
> Rhine dammed for a hydroelectric plant and coal mined for energy. The gathering that sets humans up to reveal
> everything this way is **enframing (*Gestell*)**, the essence of modern technology. The **danger** is that humans
> come to see themselves as standing-reserve too. The **"saving power"** grows where the danger is (Heidegger quotes
> Hölderlin), and art/*poiesis* is a possible way out. ***Techne*** in Greek covered **both craft and the fine arts**,
> and is a kind of knowing (linked with *episteme*), not just making. If the quiz asks about *Gestell*, it comes from
> the essay and not from this video.

## Likely quiz points

1. Author and work: **Martin Heidegger, *The Question Concerning Technology***.
2. **"The essence of technology is nothing technological."** The essence isn't any one piece of technology (tree analogy).
3. Goal: a **"free relationship"** to technology, one that **opens human existence to its essence**.
4. **"Questioning builds a way."** Humans are questioning beings (**Dasein**).
5. Standard definitions: **instrumental** (a means to an end) + **anthropological** (a human activity). **Correct but not true / not adequate.**
6. **The worst attitude** is regarding technology as **neutral**. Affirming, enduring and rejecting it also miss the essence.
7. **Will to mastery**: it grows the more technology threatens to slip out of control (using tech to control tech).
8. **"The merely correct is not yet the true."** Seek the true **by way of** the correct.
9. **"Wherever instrumentality reigns, there reigns causality"**: the step to the **four causes** (material, formal, final, efficient).
10. Example: the **silver chalice**: silver, chalice form, sacrificial rite, **silversmith**.
11. The four causes are **four ways of being responsible / indebted** (Greek ***aition***). The **silversmith is not a *causa efficiens***. He **gathers** the other three.
12. Modern thought made the **efficient cause** the model and dropped the final cause.
13. The four are unified by **occasioning → bringing-forth (*poiesis*)**, with the quote from **Plato's *Symposium***.
14. ***Physis*** has bringing-forth **in itself** (a blossom). Craft and art have it **in another** (the artisan).
15. Bringing-forth = concealment → **unconcealment = *aletheia* = truth** (Romans: ***veritas***). So **technology is a mode of revealing**. Modern technology's revealing is tied to **standing-reserve (*Bestand*)**.

## For the debate

Useful if your motion is about AI or technology:

- **"It's just a tool" is the weak point.** Heidegger grants the instrumental view is correct but calls treating
  technology as **neutral** the worst blindness. **Every new technology shifts our trajectory.** That's a strong line
  against "AI is neutral, only its use is good or bad."
- **Will to mastery**: if the answer to tech going out of control is *more* tech (screen-time apps, AI to police AI),
  ask whether that just deepens the problem.
- **Both camps can be accused of missing the point**: uncritical enthusiasm, resigned acceptance and Luddite
  rejection all stay inside "the technological". The position Heidegger argues for is a **free relationship**: use it,
  but question what it does to how we see the world and ourselves.
- Careful: **he isn't anti-technology**. The lecturer stresses he's not a "neo-Luddite" [09:23]. Don't cite him for
  "ban it".
