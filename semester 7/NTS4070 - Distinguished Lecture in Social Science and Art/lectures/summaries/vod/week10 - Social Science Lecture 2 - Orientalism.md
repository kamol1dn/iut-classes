## What the book is and why it matters [01:19]

- Said's **1978** book ***Orientalism*** "revolutionized the study of the Middle East". It helped create
  **post-colonial theory** and influenced English, history, anthropology, political science and cultural studies.
  Translated into **26 languages**, required reading at many universities, and **one of the most controversial
  scholarly books** of the last 30 years [01:31–02:08].
- **The question it asks** [02:11]: why do we already "know" what kind of people live in the Middle East, what they
  believe and how they act, **even if we've never been there or met anyone from there**? More generally, how do we
  come to understand **strangers who look different from us**?
- **The central argument** [02:35]: this knowledge is **not innocent or objective**. It's the result of a process
  that **reflects certain interests**. It is **"highly motivated"**.
- **Orientalism, defined** [03:02]: the **lens** through which the West (Europe and the US) views the Middle East.
  It **distorts** the reality of those places and people. It's a **framework for understanding the unfamiliar and
  strange** that makes those peoples **appear different and threatening**. The narrator's everyday word for the
  general process: **stereotyping** [03:15].

## 1. Where the book came from: two reasons [03:34]

| Reason | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Immediate**: the **Arab-Israeli War of 1973** | The media had said Arabs are **cowardly, can't fight, will always lose "because they're not modern"**. Then the **Egyptian army crossed the canal** in early October 1973 and showed "like anybody else, they could fight" [03:38–04:04] |
| **Lifelong**: a gap between experience and image | The **disparity** between his own experience of **being an Arab** and how Arabs were **represented in art** [04:14] |

The artists he means are **great** ones: painters **Delacroix, Ingres, Gérôme**; novelists **Disraeli, Flaubert**
[04:28–04:38]. Their Orient "had very little to do with" his background. **"So I decided to write the history of
that."** [04:49]

## 2. Orientalism as an organized body of writing [05:05]

- A Parisian or Londoner of the **1850s–60s** who wanted to write about **India, Egypt or Syria** couldn't approach
  it **"in a free and creative way"**. **A great deal of writing had gone before**, and it was **organized, "like an
  organized science"**. That body of writing is what Said calls Orientalism [05:29–05:43].
- **A repertory of images** that keeps coming back [05:47]:
  - the **sensual woman** "there to be sort of used by the man"
  - the East as a **mysterious place full of secrets and monsters**: **"the marvels of the East"**
- It's **consistent with itself** and has **little to do with people who'd actually been there**. Even travellers
  who went **didn't modify it much**. So there were **no realistic representations** of the Orient in literature,
  painting, music or any of the arts [06:04–06:23].
- It reaches into the **experts** too. **Edward William Lane** wrote his book on the **modern Egyptians** in the
  1830s, and **scholars in the 1920s say more or less the same thing** [06:33–06:50].

### The Nerval example [06:52]

The French poet **Gérard de Nerval** wrote up his **"voyage to the Orient"**. Reading his travels in **Syria**,
Said felt it sounded familiar. Then he saw that Nerval was, **almost unconsciously, quoting Lane on the
Egyptians**. The assumption behind it: **"the Orientals are all the same no matter where you find them"**: India,
Syria, Egypt, "the same essence" [07:07–07:19].

### The timeless Orient and the "Other" [07:20]

- The result is an image of the **timeless Orient**: unlike the West, it **doesn't develop**. It is **"outside of
  history"**, **"placid and still and eternal"**, which **"the fact of history"** simply contradicts [07:20–07:39].
- **"It's a creation of, you might say, an ideal other for Europe."** [07:42] Europe defines itself against an
  Orient it has imagined.

## 3. Knowledge and power: empire and Napoleon in Egypt [08:03]

- The narrator: Said doesn't only describe Orientalism's content. He argues **why it looks the way it does**, from
  its **concrete historical and institutional context**: **imperial conquest** [08:03–08:18].
- The **British and French** empires mattered most for the East. They conquered **militarily and "ideologically"**.
  Their question: **how do we understand the natives so we can conquer and subdue them more easily?** [08:27–08:44]
- Big abstract categories for people with a different skin colour are as old as contact between cultures. What's
  new is that **Orientalism makes it formal and presents it as objective knowledge** [08:46–09:04].
- **Napoleon's conquest of Egypt, 1798** is the **break** that starts the project: "the first really important
  modern imperial expedition" [09:06–09:28].
  - Unlike the **Spaniards in the New World**, he didn't come **looking for loot**. He brought soldiers **plus
    scientists, botanists, architects, philologists, biologists, historians** to **record Egypt in every
    conceivable way** [09:30–09:53].
  - The survey was made **"not for the Egyptian, but for the European"**. Its volumes are **a metre square**, and
    their size announces the **power and prestige** of a European state that can do to Egyptians **what Egyptians
    cannot do to the French**. **"There's no comparable Egyptian survey of France."** [09:55–10:20]
  - The key line: **"To produce knowledge, you have to have a power to be there, and to see in expert ways things
    that the natives themselves can't see."** [10:23]

## 4. British/French vs American Orientalism [10:45]

Different Orientalisms come from different experiences of "the Orient":

| | **British and French** | **American** |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | **direct colonial rule**: Britain in **India** for several hundred years, France in **North Africa (Algeria)** and **Indochina** | **no American occupation of the Near East** (as of 1997) |
| Character | an **"archive of actual experiences"** of being there and ruling [11:15] | **indirect, "much more based on abstractions"** [11:46] |
| Extra factor | | **politicized by Israel**, America's main ally in the region [11:59] |

- A clip of a US official (it sounds like Vice President Al Gore) boasts that the US recognised Israel **11 minutes**
  after it declared independence [12:16].
- Said: Israel, a **Jewish and self-declared Western state** in the middle of the Islamic world, is felt to share
  American interests more than **Iran or Saudi Arabia**, which matter mainly **for oil** [12:30–12:52]. Israel's view
  of the Arab world as its enemy gets **"imported into American Orientalism"** [12:54].
- His example: the idea that **Hamas** militants "are just interested in killing Jewish children", while the
  **30-year occupation of the West Bank and Gaza** ("the longest military occupation in this century", as of 1997)
  and the **dispossession of Palestinians** go unmentioned [13:06–13:37].
- So Arab struggles for **national independence** and Palestinian **self-determination** are seen as **upsetting the
  status quo**. An American can hardly find TV, books or films on the Middle East where Arabs aren't **terrorists,
  violent and irrational** [13:41–14:16].

## 5. The new American Orientalism: the Islamic terrorist [14:31]

- A short clip of someone (not Said) calling on America to face its **racism towards Muslims and Arabs** [14:31].
- The narrator [14:57]: **anti-Arab racism seems almost officially sanctioned**; you can say things about Arabs
  that **wouldn't be tolerated about any other group**. At the centre is the **demonized figure of the Islamic
  terrorist**, pushed by **journalists and Hollywood** [15:14].
- Said **does not deny terrorism exists**; it comes out of the region's violent politics. His point is that a focus
  on **one negative aspect** makes **all** peoples of the Islamic world look the same: a **threat**. "We think
  **fanatic, extreme, violent**" [15:25–15:57]. This **strips the humanity and diversity** from millions of ordinary
  people living decent lives [15:59].

## 6. *Covering Islam* and the media [16:17]

- Said's **trilogy**: ***Orientalism*** (1978), ***The Question of Palestine*** (1979) and ***Covering Islam***
  (1981) [16:17–16:28].
- *Covering Islam* came out of the **Iranian Revolution**, which called itself Islamic. He found an **"arsenal of
  images"**: crowds **waving their fists**, **black banners**, the **"stern-faced Khomeini"**. Islam came across as
  **frightening, mysterious, above all threatening**, "as if the main business of Muslims was to threaten and try to
  kill Americans" [16:39–17:26].
- **It got worse, not better.** Updating the book in **1996**, 16–17 years on, he expected more events to bring
  more familiarity and nuance. "Quite to my horror and surprise", the picture had become **more threatening**
  [17:28–18:08].
- Example: the TV film ***Jihad in America***, made after the **1993 World Trade Center bombing**. A clip plays
  [18:22–18:57].
- **Double standard** [19:01]: nobody generalised about the **Oklahoma City bombing** as "Christian
  fundamentalist". But "Islamic jihad had come to America".
- **Irresponsible journalism**: footage of people **speaking Arabic** with a **voiceover** saying they're
  discussing the destruction of America, when anyone who **knew the language** could tell it had **nothing to do
  with that** [19:10–19:25].
- Islam became **"synonymous with terror"**, with **"very little distinction between piety and violence"** [19:27].
- **Why the media do it** [19:39]: the "so-called independent media" in a liberal society are **lazy** and
  controlled by **commercial and political interests**. No investigative reporting, just **repeating the
  government line**. For the powerful, **Islam is "a useful foreign demon"** that turns attention **away from
  inequities at home** [20:14].
- Net result: the **human side** of the Islamic and Arab world is **missing**, a **"vacancy"**, filled by **easy,
  almost automatic images of terror** [20:29–20:46].

## 7. Hollywood's repertory [20:49]

- The film plays the opening song of Disney's ***Aladdin*** (1992), which presents the Arab homeland as
  **barbaric** [21:01].
- Said loved Hollywood **Arabian Nights** films as a boy in **Palestine and Cairo**, with **Jon Hall, Maria Montez
  and Sabu**. They showed the world he lived in, but with an **"exotic, magical quality"** that was pure Hollywood
  [21:29–21:56]. The stock kit: **sheikhs, desert, galloping, scimitars, dancing girls** [21:56].
- Today Muslims in popular media are **two things**: **(a) villains and fanatics** (a film villain threatens to send
  Americans "to the hell they deserve" [22:14]), and **(b) bodies**: many films end with **heaps of dead Muslims**
  killed by **Arnold Schwarzenegger, Demi Moore or Chuck Norris** [22:25–22:39]. **"So the idea of Islam is
  something to be stamped out."**
- The old Orientalist idea underneath: the Muslim / Oriental as **"a lesser breed"** who **"only understand the
  language of force"**. "Unless you give them a bloody nose, they won't understand. We can't talk reason with them"
  [23:03–23:19].

## 8. "Is the Arab world full of terrorists?" Against generalization [23:30]

- Common sense: **there are terrorists, as there are everywhere**, but there's a lot more going on. We're talking
  about **250–300 million people** [23:32–23:46].
- **"One of the great problems with Orientalism ... is these vast generalizations about Islam and the nature of
  Islam."** [23:50] **Indonesia and Saudi Arabia** are both Muslim, but differ so much in history, language and
  tradition that "the word Islam has, at best, a **tenuous meaning**" [23:58–24:15].
- The same inside the Arab world: **Morocco ≠ Saudi Arabia, Algeria ≠ Egypt**. And he argues **the predominant mood
  of the Arab world is very secular** [24:18–24:31].
- **Why the threat is useful** [24:33]: after the **Cold War**, there have to be **"foreign devils"**, otherwise
  what is the **huge military budget** for? "So you have to have threat." [25:01] (His figure as transcribed, "twice
  as much as the entire world's military budget combined", can't be literally right. Don't quote the number.)
- Islam is cast as **the enemy of Christianity**; the US sees itself as **Judeo-Christian**, allied with Israel,
  with Islam as **"the great enemy, the competitor"** [25:05–25:19].

## 9. Discourse: why you can't simply think past it [25:20]

- **The Dodi Fayed example** [25:20]: just before Princess **Diana**'s companion Dodi Fayed died (1997), the English
  press was "full of the racist clichés of **Orientalist discourse**". ***The Sunday Times*** ran a 15,000-word story
  headlined **"A Match Made in Mecca"**, imagining **Muslim conspiracies** taking over a **white woman**, with the
  7th-century **Prophet Muhammad** somehow stage-managing it [25:27–25:58].
- **"That's the power of the discourse, you see."** [26:02] If you think about Islam and that part of the world,
  **"those are the words you constantly have to use."**
- **The definition** (key line): **"The discourse is a regulated system of producing knowledge within certain
  constraints whereby certain rules have to be observed."** [26:21]
- **"To think past it, to go beyond it, not to use it, is virtually impossible because there's no knowledge that
  isn't codified in this way about that part of the world."** [26:35] (Film clips of Arab villains are cut in
  around this.)

## 10. The Arabs' own part in it [27:02]

Said also blames **Arab governments**. By **not mounting a serious critique**, the Arabs **"participated and
continue to allow themselves to be represented as Orientals"** [27:07–27:18].

- The **22 Arab countries** have **no information policy** to present their ways of life differently, because they
  are **dictatorships "without democracy"** that **need US patronage**. So they won't criticise the US or have a
  real dialogue [27:21–27:47].
- So the Arabs keep themselves **"subordinate"** and **"inferior"**, which **fulfils the very representations**
  Westerners already hold [27:50–28:04].

## 11. Oklahoma City, 1995: Orientalism in real time [28:22]

- News clips: a US government source says the attack has **"Middle East terrorism written all over it"**; a
  commentator calls mass casualties **"a Middle Eastern trait"**; the FBI asks for **Arabic speakers**; a bulletin
  goes out for **three men "of Middle Eastern origin"** [28:22–30:35].
- Said calls it an example of the **"almost astonishing persistence"** of Orientalism [28:58]. He was lecturing in
  **Canada**. About half an hour after the bombing, his office had **25 calls** from networks, cable channels,
  newspapers and magazines, all wanting to talk to **him** [29:16–29:53].
- Why? An "instant commentator" had said it **looked like a Middle East–style bombing** and mentioned "a couple of
  **swarthy** people" nearby. So the media assumed that **being from the Middle East** gave him **insight** into it
  [30:00–30:44].
- **"The proposition is so preposterous and so racist."** It was a **"local homegrown boy called McVeigh"**,
  "totally American in his outlook", acting out of **"Ahab-like anger"** at the world [30:49–31:09].

## 12. Said the Palestinian: the link between work and politics [31:24]

- The narrator: Said is a **literary theorist** and also a prominent **representative of the Palestinian people**.
  He grew up in Palestine. In **1948**, when Israel was founded, he and his family, like millions of Palestinians,
  were made **homeless and stateless** [31:24–31:48].
- His three books form one project. A just solution in the Middle East (**"the question of Palestine"**) needs
  **overcoming Orientalism's racist legacy**, which **separates people** and treats **difference as a threat to be
  contained or destroyed** [32:22–32:38].
- **Palestine-Israel is "the ultimate test case"** for the 21st century: **live together** in peace with our
  differences, or **live apart in fear and loathing**, constantly at war [32:43–32:57].

## 13. Gramsci and the "inventory" [33:04]

- The way out draws on the Italian philosopher **Antonio Gramsci**, from his ***Prison Notebooks*** [33:07–33:18].
- **History deposits in us** our own, our family's, our nation's and our tradition's history: **"an infinity of
  traces"**, through heredity, collective, individual and family experience. **"But there's no inventory"**, no
  orderly guide [33:27–33:58].
- **So the first task is to compile an inventory**: to make sense of it. For Said this is **"the most interesting
  sort of human task"**, the **task of interpretation**, of giving history **shape and sense** [34:02–34:18].
- The **purpose** matters. **Not** to show my history is better or worse than yours, or "I'm a victim and you're
  the oppressor", but **to understand my history in terms of other people's history** and generalise from one's
  own experience to others' [34:23–34:40].
- **The great goal** [34:44]: **"to become someone else"**, to move **"from a unitary identity to an identity that
  includes the other without suppressing the difference."** Understand yourself in relation to others, and
  **understand others as you would understand yourself** [35:14–35:18].

## 14. Coexistence [35:22]

- **Palestine as a mosaic**: Arab Muslims, Arab Christians, and Israeli Jews of very mixed backgrounds (**Polish,
  Russian, American, Yemeni, Iraqi, Indian Jews**) on a land **holy to three major religions**, fought over for
  thousands of years [35:22–36:09].
- **Two patterns**:

| | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **The Zionist pattern** (Said's description) [36:09] | "it was promised to us, we're the chosen people", everyone else second-rate: **throw them out or make them second-class citizens** |
| **The other Palestinian vision** [36:23] | Palestinians are made to pay for the **Holocaust**, "an entirely Christian and European catastrophe in which the Arabs played no part". **"We've become the victims of the victims."** But many don't answer with "throw them out because we were thrown out". They propose **coexistence**: Muslim, Christian and Jew in one **polity**, replacing **"the authoritarian hierarchical model"** [36:48–37:17] |

- **"The most dangerous idea at the end of the 20th century"**: that we must **protect ourselves against the
  "infiltrations, the infections of the other"** [37:20–37:29]. Without another way: **wholesale violence** like
  the **Gulf War, Bosnia, the Rwandan massacres** [37:38–37:47].
- **The challenge is coexistence**: how do we live with people of **different religions, traditions and languages**
  in the same polity? **"How do we accept difference without violence and hostility?"** [37:56–38:18]

## 15. The closing image: contrapuntal lines [38:24]

Said's field, **comparative literature**, doesn't aim to rank literatures (English as a poor cousin of French, or
Arabic of Persian, "any of those silly things"). It shows them existing as **"contrapuntal lines in a great
composition"**, where **difference is respected and understood without coercion**. **"It's that attitude, I think,
that we need."** [38:41–38:55]

## Likely to surprise or challenge

Hooks to react to, not answers:

1. **Knowledge isn't innocent.** What "everyone knows" about a region can be built by people who never went there,
   and it serves someone's interests.
2. **Great art can carry stereotypes.** Delacroix, Flaubert and Nerval are masterpieces *and* part of the problem;
   Nerval was copying Lane without realising it.
3. **"To produce knowledge, you have to have a power to be there."** There's no Egyptian survey of France. Who gets
   to describe whom?
4. **The timeless Orient.** The idea that the East "doesn't develop" while the West does. Central Asia was painted
   exactly this way by the Russian Empire.
5. **Arabic speech with a false voiceover.** If you can't understand the language, you have to trust the framing.
   How often does that happen with news you watch?
6. **Oklahoma City.** Within hours, the media "knew" it was Middle Eastern, and called Said because of where he was
   from. The bomber was American.
7. **Said blames the Arab dictatorships too.** By staying silent they help confirm the stereotype. Self-Orientalism
   is uncomfortable to think about.
8. **"Victims of the victims"** and Gramsci's inventory: understand your history *in terms of* others', not to win
   a victimhood contest. Is that possible for any nation?

## For the debate

- **Watch your generalizations.** Said's main charge is **vast generalization** ("Islam", "the Arabs", "the
  East"). An opponent who says "all X countries" or "people like that" can be answered with his
  **Indonesia ≠ Saudi Arabia** point [23:58]. Make sure your own claims about "the West", "Asia" or
  "developing countries" are just as specific.
- **Ask who is framing the evidence.** The rubric rewards **facts and statistics**. Said's lesson is to check
  **who produced them, for whom, and with what interest** (Napoleon's survey, the government line repeated by "lazy"
  media). It's a strong question in cross-examination.
- **Useful if your topic touches media, national identity, tourism or how Uzbekistan is seen abroad**: being
  represented vs representing yourself, and his point that weak governments with **no information policy** leave
  the image to others [27:21].
- Careful: **this is a 1997 interview with a very openly political speaker.** His numbers (the military budget,
  "longest occupation") are claims made then. Use his framework, not his figures.
