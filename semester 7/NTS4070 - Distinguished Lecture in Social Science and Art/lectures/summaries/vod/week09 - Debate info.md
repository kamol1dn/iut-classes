## Splitting 15 minutes [05:16]

His worked example for **five members**:

1. Three speakers, **3–4 minutes each**, about **10–12 minutes** in total.
2. That leaves about **3 minutes**, shared between member 4's **question to the other team** and member 5's **answer** to
   theirs.

For **4 or 6 members** the limit is still 15 minutes, so plan around the clock [06:07]:

- **Six members:** split the speeches more finely, e.g. **four claims instead of three**.
- **Four members:** two or three speakers can make **two big claims (theses)**, and the rest defend. The **same person may
  both ask and defend**.

How you organise it is up to you. The only fixed rule is that **everyone gets a role** [07:02].

## What each criterion means, in his words [08:03]

Each criterion is worth **6 points**.

| Criterion | What he stressed |
|---|---|
| **Understanding of topic** | Show you **did your homework**. Don't turn up having just read the motion and its title |
| **Organization of arguments** | The argument construction from the textbook lectures: **tie every argument to the main premise**, tight and logical. "The frame" of the argument is what's graded |
| **Use of facts / statistics** | **Don't just make claims.** Back each one with real evidence or real-life examples, and **statistics are better still** |
| **Defense** | How well the designated member **answers the other side's question** |
| **Presentation style** | Tone, clarity, **respect for the other team**, good behaviour, **professional dress** |
