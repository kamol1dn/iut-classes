## Opening case: Shannon Townsend [00:03]

Shannon had a 3.9 GPA after her first year at the University of Colorado and
planned to become a physician. She then dropped out to join a nomadic cult, the
Jim Roberts Group ("the Brethren"), which cuts members off from family and
possessions. Her family hasn't seen her since. His point: cults target young
people in transition, and they rely on fallacious reasoning that hides their
real goals. **Asking questions and staying informed** is the defence, because
such groups dislike people who reason.

## What a fallacy is [04:07]

- **Fallacy:** a faulty argument that *at first appears to be correct*.
- **Formal fallacy:** the **form** of the argument is invalid. Example: "Some
  dropouts are men; no doctors are dropouts; therefore no doctors are men."
- **Informal fallacy:** the argument is **psychologically or emotionally
  persuasive but logically incorrect.** These are today's topic.

There are three families of informal fallacy. **Know which family each one
belongs to.** That is the obvious exam structure.

## 1. Fallacies of ambiguity [06:12]

These come from ambiguous words, sloppy grammar, or confusing two related concepts.

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example from the lecture | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equivocation** | A key term **changes meaning** partway through the argument. Also covers misused **relative terms** (tall, big, good) | "No animals allowed… humans are animals too." "Katie (age 2) is very tall, her dad is average, so Katie is taller than her dad." | Define ambiguous terms first. Don't reuse relative terms across contexts. |
| **Amphiboly** | A **grammatical** ambiguity allows more than one conclusion | "Mary's mother and her husband have opposing views." The ad slogan "Wear it **and** be happy": is that a causal link, or two unrelated ideas? | Use grammar carefully. Ask the speaker to rephrase. |
| **Accent** | The meaning shifts depending on **which word is stressed** | "Crack down on *off-campus* drinking" → "so on-campus is fine." "Don't *play* with matches" → "I was burning down Mr Murphy's shed." | Ask which word was meant to carry the emphasis. |
| **Division** | A trait of the **whole group** is assumed for **each member** | "Canadians are nice, so Derek from Vancouver must be nice." National stereotypes. | Judge the individual. |

## 2. Fallacies of relevance [14:21]

The premise is **logically irrelevant** to the conclusion but *feels* relevant.

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal attack (ad hominem)** | Attacking the **person** instead of answering the argument | Political debates, comment sections |
| **Appeal to force (scare tactics)** | A physical, psychological or **legal** threat used to make someone back down | A parent: "I pay your tuition, so you don't get to disagree." **AI CEOs** (he names Sam Altman and Dario Amodei) warning that AI will doom us within a decade. He asks you to think about *why* they make such claims. |
| **Appeal to pity** | Evoking pity where it is **irrelevant** | Telling a police officer who stopped you for speeding that something bad happened at home |
| **Popular appeal** | True "because everyone believes it". Includes the **bandwagon** and **snob appeal** (celebrity endorsements) | "God must exist; most people believe in God." Footballers advertising products |
| **Appeal to ignorance** | True because nobody has proven it false, or false because nobody has proven it true. "Ignorance" means lacking the means of proof, **not** stupidity | "UFOs don't exist since no one has proved they do." **Exception: in court**, the defendant is presumed innocent and the burden of proof lies with the prosecution. |
| **Hasty generalisation** | Generalising from a sample that is **too small or biased** | "All men are abusers," drawn from abusive relatives. Muslims scapegoated after 9/11 |
| **Straw man** | **Distorting** the opponent's argument so it's easier to refute | "Mamdani wants free transit and to tax the rich, so socialism, and socialism failed in the USSR." **Avoid it** by going back to the original argument: has it been reworded or oversimplified, or have key parts been dropped? |
| **Red herring** | **Side-tracking** to a different issue | "Why are you upset about me driving after a few drinks? Look at the accidents caused by phone use." The name comes from UK fox hunting: a smelly herring was used to throw the hounds off the scent. |

## 3. Fallacies involving unwarranted assumptions [30:45]

The argument rests on an assumption that **no evidence supports**, which weakens the whole argument.

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Begging the question** (circular reasoning) | The conclusion just **rewords a premise** | "Capital punishment is wrong because it's immoral to inflict death as punishment." Hard to spot at first. |
| **Inappropriate appeal to authority** | Citing an expert from a **different field** | Celebrities and bloggers on podcasts giving advice on politics, family or money. Experts from one IT sub-field speaking about another. |
| **Loaded question** | The question **presumes the answer** to an unasked question | "Have you stopped beating your wife?" in court, demanding yes or no |
| **False dilemma** | A complex issue reduced to **either/or** | "If you don't like our policies, leave the country." |
| **Questionable cause (post hoc)** | Assuming that because A came **before** B, A **caused** B | Lucky jacket, lucky route to the exam. Avoiding people with **non-infectious** conditions out of unfamiliarity. "Correlation ≠ causation." |
| **Slippery slope** | Allow one thing and **every extreme version** follows | "Let your child do X and they'll end up spoiled or criminal." "Listen to young people once and they'll take over and strike." |
| **Naturalistic fallacy** | Natural = good, unnatural = bad | Tobacco is natural but harmful. Hurricanes and earthquakes are natural. "Hunting is natural, so hunting rare animals is fine." |

## Strategies for avoiding fallacies [43:00]

1. **Know yourself.** Learn which fallacies you tend to fall for or commit.
2. **Build self-confidence and self-esteem.** Confident people back down less and get less defensive.
3. **Cultivate good listening.** Stay respectful even when you disagree.
4. **Avoid ambiguous terms and faulty grammar.** Define key terms, and ask when something is unclear.
5. **Don't confuse an argument's soundness with the person making it.** His
   caveat: in criminal investigations a person's history *can* be relevant.
6. **Know your topic.** Research before concluding.
7. **Be sceptical without closing your mind.** That includes scepticism of your own views.
8. **Watch your body language.** Ad hominem and appeals to force can be
   non-verbal: eye-rolling, glaring, walking away.
9. **Don't be set on "winning".** Arguing to win rather than to find the truth
   pushes you toward fallacies.
