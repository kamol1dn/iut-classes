## Why science matters for a critical thinker [00:40]

Global warming, COVID, **nuclear energy in Uzbekistan**, AI and vaccines all have conflicting narratives backed by
"research". So where is the truth? You need tools to interpret scientific claims.

## 1. What science is [02:50]

**Science** reasons from **observable, measurable facts (data)** to **testable explanations**. Scientists discover,
observe and collect facts systematically and explain the relationships between them. Science is also **a human-made
system** that rests on a worldview and a set of assumptions:

| Assumption | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Empiricism** | the physical senses are the primary source of knowledge. More data means better explanations |
| **Objectivity** | we can study the world as an object outside us without bias, so systematic observation leads scientists to agree |
| **Materialism** | everything is physical matter. (**Dualism**, belief in a separate spiritual realm, is the minority view.) *Not* "materialistic" in the consumer sense |
| **Predictability** | the universe is orderly: causal relationships, discoverable by observation and **inductive** reasoning |
| **Unity** | an underlying unified dynamic structure runs through all phenomena |

### Limitations [09:10]

- **Descartes**: we only have an *idea* of the outside world, not direct evidence that it exists.
- **Empiricism** limits science to what can be observed and shared. **Dark matter, dark energy**, some EM waves and
  particles escape our senses and instruments.
- **Quantum mechanics** questions orderliness and predictability.
- **Hume's point** [11:50]: however often B follows A, observation can't prove a *necessary* causal connection,
  and it can't reveal an underlying unity.
- Experience depends on **how our brains organize** sense input. Example: what people "saw" on the Moon or planets
  before telescopes and space missions.

## 2. Science and religion: four positions [13:20]

| # | Position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Science trumps religion** when they conflict | most scientists and Western philosophers. It created antagonism with **fundamentalist** religion (scripture as the literal word of God) |
| 2 | **Religion trumps science** | the opposite camp, partly a reaction to #1 |
| 3 | **Separate, mutually exclusive realms** | science asks *how/what* (objective, empirical), religion asks *why* (values, spiritual). You can accept evolution while ensoulment stays spiritual. **Problem**: on some historical claims they *do* clash, and both can't be true |
| 4 | **Same reality, compatible and complementary** | if scripture conflicts with science, **reinterpret scripture**. They look at different aspects of one reality, so they must ultimately converge and inform each other |

## 3. The scientific method [20:55]

It parallels the earlier **three levels of thinking** (experience → interpretation → analysis).

1. **Identify the problem.** Do a **literature review** (Google Scholar, journal databases) to find a **gap**.
   Useful advice if you're heading for a master's or PhD.
2. **Develop an initial hypothesis**, a proposed explanation of the phenomena.
   A **scientific theory** is an explanation backed by well-substantiated facts, laws and *tested* hypotheses
   (relativity, or his field's **human-capital theory**: more education → higher earnings) [24:40].
3. **Gather more information and refine** the hypothesis.
4. **Test it** in a lab, a simulation or an experiment.
5. **Evaluate it** against the results: right, wrong, or a new contribution.

## 4. Evaluating a hypothesis [28:00]

A good hypothesis is:

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Relevant** | tied to the phenomenon. A vague problem breeds off-topic hypotheses |
| **Consistent with well-established theories** | gives you validation from the research community |
| **Simple** | among rivals that meet the criteria, pick the simpler (Occam's razor). His example: most scientists prefer **evolution over intelligent design** partly because it's simpler. But truly new ideas are often *not* simple |
| **Testable and falsifiable** | replicable by others. Since 100% proof is impossible, **falsifiability, not confirmation**, is the test. *"All swans are white"* fell to **one black swan** [32:50] |
| **Predictive / fruitful** | the **Big Bang** predicted expansion, **helium abundance** and galaxy distribution. Human-capital theory (US, 1960s) can be tested in Uzbekistan |

**Pseudoscience** masquerades as science to gain legitimacy, but rests on **emotional appeals, superstition and
rhetoric** instead of method: numerology, mind reading, astrology, psychic healing [35:00].

## 5. Research methodology [36:50]

A systematic approach to gathering and analysing information. **Designs**: experimental (intervention, simulation,
lab), correlational, survey, ethnographic, grounded theory, narrative, action, **mixed methods** (e.g. a survey, then
in-depth interviews with some respondents). There are two methodologies:
**quantitative** (numbers) vs **qualitative** (meaning: interviews, document analysis) [39:00].

## 6. Ethics in science [39:40]

Prompted by the **Milgram** and **Stanford Prison** experiments from the early lectures:

- **Data collection**: **informed consent**, full knowledge of the consequences, respect for site, participants and norms, animal experiments.
- **Data reporting**: honest reporting (no skewing towards your hypothesis or politics), **proper citation**, no plagiarism or translated copying,
  **publishing on time** (findings that could help people shouldn't sit unpublished).
- **"Publish or perish"** [43:05]: KPIs on paper counts mean stress, neglected teaching and shallow science. His
  hypothetical: if IUT demanded 10 papers a year from everyone. **Einstein** wrote roughly 300 works over 50 years, about 5–6 a year,
  while some top scientists today publish 50–100 a year. Is that moving science, or playing the game? It also tempts academics to use AI themselves.
- **Neutrality of science**: political pressure and private funding. Always ask **who funded the study** ("we're close to
  AGI" from AI-company-backed research) [47:30].

## 7. Thomas Kuhn: *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) [49:10]

Kuhn challenged the view that science is **objective** and **progressive**. Science is a **social construct**, biased
by social expectations and professional norms about what counts as an acceptable hypothesis.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Normal science** | research built on past achievements that a community accepts as its foundation. It lives in journals and textbooks |
| **Paradigm** | the accepted view of what the world is like and how to study it. It shapes **what counts as a problem** and even **what we perceive**. It becomes normal science when it solves problems *and* attracts followers |
| **Scientific revolution / paradigm shift** | progress is **not linear**. Persistent **anomalies** cause a **crisis**, and a new theory replaces the old paradigm |

Examples: **Copernicus** (heliocentrism), **Alfred Wegener**'s continental drift (ignored at first, accepted 30–40 years
later). New paradigms win over **young scientists and outsiders** who aren't invested in the old one. **Einstein was 26**
when he published relativity in 1905 [54:40].
