## Opening: the Lincoln–Douglas debates (1858) [02:07]

A film clip of the Illinois Senate debates on slavery and the Dred Scott decision.
Lincoln lost the election but emerged as a nationally known speaker and critical
thinker. The **format** (argument, then counter-argument and rebuttal) is still
used in scientific and political debate, and it's the model for your own debates.

**"Argument" in logic doesn't mean a quarrel.** It means *using reason and evidence to support a claim*.

## 1. Identify the issue [12:22]

- An **issue** is an ill-defined set of problems involving controversy or uncertainty.
- A vaguely defined issue lets the debate slide sideways and lets the other side drag you off-topic.
- **Ask the right questions.** How you word the issue shapes the solution.
  Lincoln **reframed** slavery from a question of state sovereignty into one of the nation's survival.
- **Wallet example:** "Who stole my wallet?" is a **loaded question** (last
  week's fallacy). The neutral version is "**What happened to my wallet?**"
  Open-mindedness is the trait of good critical thinkers, and of good detectives.

## 2. Rhetoric vs argument [16:27]

| Rhetoric | Argument |
|---|---|
| Starts from a **position** already taken | Starts from an **open question** |
| Aim: **persuade** | Aim: **discover the truth** |
| Tends to polarise into two sides, us good and them evil | Open to **many** perspectives; resists two-sided framing |

Good arguments give the audience **reasons**, are pitched at that audience, and **invite feedback**.

## 3. Parts of an argument [20:29]

- **Proposition:** a statement expressing a complete thought that is **true or false**.
  "The Earth revolves around the Sun" is true. "Toronto is the capital of Canada" is false.
- **Conclusion:** the proposition being supported. Also called a claim, viewpoint
  or position. It can appear **anywhere**: at the start as a thesis, or at the end.
- **Premise:** a proposition that gives a reason for the conclusion. Good premises rest on **fact and experience, not opinion or assumption**.
  Example: "Ottawa is the capital of Canada [premise], so Toronto isn't [conclusion]."

**Four types of premise:**

| Type | Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | Empirical fact: observation, the five senses, science | — |
| **Prescriptive** | Value statements | "It's wrong to cheat on exams." "We should strive for campus diversity." |
| **Analogical** | A comparison | Plato: reason should rule the passions as a charioteer rules his horses |
| **Definitional** | Defines a key term | What exactly do we mean by "diversity"? |

**These are not arguments:**
- **Explanation:** says why or how something happened. We already know it happened.
  Example: "The cat yelled because I stepped on her tail."
- **Conditional (if–then):** "If you come from Russia, then you speak Russian."
  On its own, no conclusion follows from it.

## 4. Breaking down an argument [26:35]

1. **Find the propositions.** Bracket them: [I think] therefore [I am].
2. **Find the conclusion.** Look for **conclusion indicators**: *therefore, thus,
   hence, so, which shows that, for these reasons, consequently, it follows that*.
3. **Find the premises.** Look for **premise indicators**: *because, since, for,
   given that, may be inferred from, the reason is that, as shown by, in view of*.

**Optional practice [30:41]:** break down *"Students who sit in the front of a class
generally earn higher grades. Therefore, you should move up to the front of the
class, since I know you want to improve your GPA."* Find its propositions, its premises and its
conclusion. Then make up your own examples with the same structure.

## 5. Evaluating an argument [30:41]

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **Clarity** | Is it clear and unambiguous? If a key term is vague, ask. "Know the battleground." |
| **Credibility** | Are the premises backed by evidence? Watch for **assumptions passed off as facts**, especially culturally popular ones or ones from non-authorities. |
| **Relevance** | Do the premises actually support the conclusion? "Most doctors were men when my daughter was young" is relevant but **insufficient** to conclude she shouldn't become a doctor. |
| **Completeness** | Are there **unstated premises**? Example: "Funding by state size, so Texas gets more than Rhode Island" relies on the unstated "Texas is bigger". That's obvious to Americans, not to someone in Uzbekistan. |
| **Soundness** | Are the premises **true** and do they **support** the conclusion? A **sound** argument has both. If the premises don't support it, the conclusion isn't proven *false*, only unknown. Example: consciousness in other people. |

## 6. Constructing an argument [38:52]

1. **State the issue clearly, in neutral terms.** "Should the USA have stricter gun
   control laws?", not "Should the government do more to keep guns from criminals?"
2. **List premises.** Set your opinions aside, avoid the two-sides trap, brainstorm with others, use reliable sources, and stay sceptical.
3. **Drop weak or irrelevant premises**, but don't drop ones just because they disagree with you.
4. **Only then, draw the conclusion** that takes in as many premises as possible.
5. **Organise it.** In an essay or talk, put the issue and the **thesis statement** (ideally one sentence) up front, with a hook about why it matters.
6. **Try it out on others.** Be open-minded and listen.
7. **Revise.** If a counter-argument is compelling, it would be irrational *not* to change your position.
8. **Act on it**, where appropriate. Critical thinking has a behavioural part: for example, write to officials *with* a realistic solution and offer to help.

- The four premise types, with an example of each.
- The five evaluation criteria, and what "sound" means.
