## The framing case: Peter Reilly, 1973

An 18-year-old high-school senior came home from church to find his mother
murdered and called the police himself. There was no physical evidence against
him — but police suspected him anyway, because his mother was locally known as a
difficult woman who took pleasure in belittling people, including her son.

What happened next is the point:

1. He **waived his right to a lawyer**, believing that telling the truth would get him released.
2. A **rotating team** of officers interrogated him, repeatedly suggesting he had flown into a rage.
3. When he denied it, they urged him to **dig deeper into his memory**.
4. **About 16 hours in**, he began to "remember" details of committing the murder.
5. Hours later — exhausted and confused — he **confessed**.

The confession was the most compelling evidence at trial and he was convicted.
He was freed after two years, when evidence emerged placing him elsewhere at the
time. The police department still believed he was guilty; the real killers were
never found.

**The lesson:** social expectation and leading questions can rewrite a person's
beliefs and even their memories — including memories of things they did not do.

## 1. Human knowledge and its limits

**Knowledge** = information or experience we believe true *and* for which we have
justification.

| Position | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Rationalism** | Most knowledge comes through reason. Plato: there is an unchanging truth reachable by reason, and most people confuse truth with worldly appearance. |
| **Empiricism** | Truth is discovered primarily through the physical senses — *empirical evidence*. This became science's main tool: data, interviews, field observation. |
| **Kant** | Rejects both. How we experience reality depends on **the structure of our own minds**. |

Kant's analogy, which he extends: a computer needs the right hardware to accept
and process particular inputs — and so does a brain. Most psychologists and
neurologists now agree that **we do not perceive reality directly**. The mind
supplies structure and rules for processing what comes in, so everything arrives
through a lens built from your past experience and worldview.

This is the foundation for everything that follows: if perception is
constructive rather than recording, then evidence needs checking — including
your own.

## 2. Evaluating evidence

**Evidence** = something that tends to prove or disprove a view.

### Sources, from least to most reliable

**Direct experience — less reliable than it feels.** The brain organises and
interprets rather than records. Memories are far less stable than scientists
once assumed. A study four years after the 1986 **Challenger disaster** found
witnesses had dramatically altered their memories, to the point of recalling
things that never happened. His everyday analogy is the whisper game, where a
message degrades as it passes along a chain.

**False memory syndrome.** After only **three sessions** of repeated suggestion
about a non-existent childhood event — being lost in a mall, spilling a drink on
a guest — roughly **a quarter of people begin to recall it**. And there is
little connection between how confident someone is in a memory and whether it is
true. The defence: pay attention to events as they happen, and stay alert to
inconsistencies in your own recollections.

**Hearsay** — heard by one person and repeated onward. Notoriously unreliable;
this is gossip.

**Anecdotal evidence** — personal testimony. Unreliable both from faulty memory
and from the human tendency to exaggerate or reshape experience to fit
expectation. His example is UFO sightings: **sincerity of belief is not
evidence**, however many people report it.

**Experts — the most credible source, with caveats.** The expert must be
knowledgeable *in the relevant field*. Citing an expert from a different field
is the fallacy of **appeal to inappropriate authority** (covered in a later
chapter).

Credentials to check:

| Check | Note |
|---|---|
| Education / training | From a reputable institution |
| Experience | Making judgments in that field |
| **Reputation among peers** | He is emphatic: *not* YouTube, blogs, Telegram channels, or follower counts — recognition by other experts |
| Accomplishments | Academic papers, actual research |

And experts are still not foolproof. They make mistakes. They **disagree** — in
which case reserve judgment or consult others. And they can be **biased**,
particularly when funded by business, government or an interest group. The
book's example: the long-assumed claim that dairy maintains strong bones,
which he says was not supported by research and was largely promoted by groups
financially dependent on dairy sales.

### Testing the evidence itself

- Credible evidence is **consistent with other relevant evidence**.
- The **more** evidence supporting a claim, the more reasonable it is to accept.
- When credible evidence is unavailable, **look for contradictory evidence** instead.

**Confirmation bias** returns here from Chapter 1, sharper: the tendency to seek
only evidence confirming your view and resist what contradicts it — strong
enough that people disregard or reinterpret contrary evidence outright.
Politicians and marketers cherry-pick this way. Its subtler form is
**scrutinising contrary evidence more rigorously than supporting evidence** —
applying an uneven standard rather than ignoring anything.

### Where to actually research

In an age of information overload, he frames the core skill as telling reliable
sources from unreliable ones.

- **Mass media** — consider the source and its slant.
- **Novels, poetry, editorials** are not intended as factual. Historical films and novels mix real figures with distortion and creative licence — his examples are Amir Temur and Genghis Khan. Real person, real battles, unreliable film.
- **Interview an expert** — faculty or outside. Take notes rather than relying on memory, and repeat back what you think you heard if unsure. They can point you to reputable publications.
- **Wikipedia** — not reliable in itself, but **go to the sources cited at the bottom** and check whether the claim is supported or invented.
- **Scholarly journals** — peer-reviewed by other experts.
- **Government documents and international organisations** (UN, UNESCO, WTO) — among the most reliable. Go to the **primary source** rather than secondary reporting on it; his example is Uzbekistan's statistics office publishing population data directly.
- **Google Scholar** for empirical research — but it is open, and some researchers chase publication counts, so evaluate the journal too. He points to **Scimago** for journal rankings.

## 3. Cognitive and perceptual errors

Perception is easily skewed by social influence. The slide links Michael Bach's
optical-illusion collection — <https://michaelbach.de/ot/> — and he suggests
playing with it.

The mind is **not a blank sheet or a recording device**. The brain constructs a
picture the way a painter does — filtering perceptions and filling in missing
information partly from expectation.

| Error | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Perceptual distortion** | A straight stick looks bent in water. The **moon illusion** — a full moon appears much larger near the horizon. |
| **Misperception of random data** | The brain dislikes absence of meaning, so it imposes patterns where none exist: shapes in clouds, lights in the sky, the "man in the moon." Hence: stay skeptical, don't conclude from what you think you saw. |
| **Stress and preconception** | Walking alone at night, a shadow becomes a person or an animal. |
| **Memorable events error** | Brain channels screen out mundane daily incidents while outstanding events stay vivid. You may not recall yesterday's meal but remember detail around an earthquake or fire ten years ago — which skews your sense of what is typical. |
| **Probability error** | Misestimating a probability by a wide margin. His example: the chance that two people in your class share a birthday — most guess far too low. |
| **Gambler's error** | Believing previous outcomes affect the probability of a random event. Lucky numbers. |

**Self-serving biases** — three forms: the misperception that we are in control;
overestimating ourselves relative to others; and exaggerating our strengths
while minimising our weaknesses.

He develops the first before the video ends. We are predisposed to believe we
control events that are outside our control, which shows up as superstition — a
lucky shirt for a big game, a charm or ritual before an exam, particular foods
or phrases. His even-handed point: these genuinely can raise confidence and calm
nerves, so they are not simply irrational. **But carried too far, the belief that
you are in control distorts thinking and produces bad decisions.**
