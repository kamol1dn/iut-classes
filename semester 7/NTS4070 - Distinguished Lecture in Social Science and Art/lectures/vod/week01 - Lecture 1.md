## The argument of the lecture

He builds Chapter 1 as a case *for* critical thinking by first showing the cost
of its absence, then defining it, then mapping how people mature into it, and
finally cataloguing what gets in the way.

### 1. Opening cases — obedience to authority

**Eichmann (1960).** The Nazi war criminal tried for crimes against humanity.
His defence was that he was following his superiors' orders. The court convicted
him anyway. The question the lecture hangs on: was he a monster, or an ordinary
man following orders?

**The Milgram experiment (Yale, 1961)** — [embedded video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vuMt8b4UrcI)**.** Designed to test whether ordinary
people would inflict harm on command. A volunteer "teacher" was told to deliver
escalating shocks to a "student" (an actor) for wrong answers, up to 450 V. When
the teacher hesitated, the experimenter used four escalating scripted prompts.
Milgram's colleagues predicted almost nobody would comply.

> **Results: 100% went to at least 300 V; 65% went all the way to 450 V.**

Criticised as unethical for deceiving participants, but replicated many times
across different populations with similar findings.

**The Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971)** — [embedded video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iXv91xFipLM)**.** 24 screened students split
by coin flip into guards and prisoners in a mock prison. Planned for two weeks,
**terminated after six days**. Guards abused their power within a day; several
prisoners broke down and were released early. It ended only after Zimbardo's
girlfriend visited and confronted him.

He includes the modern critique rather than presenting it as settled: recordings
show the guards were **coached** to be tough, the best-known distressed prisoner
later described it as an improv exercise, and the prison consultant said the
crueler treatments were not spontaneous. Many now call it drama rather than
experiment.

**The takeaway he draws:** people will uncritically follow those in power. He
then makes it personal — asking students to recall times a superior asked
something against their own moral standards, what they did, and *how they
justified it either way*. Likely debate or midterm material.

### 2. Definitions

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Critical thinking** | A *collection* of skills used daily, necessary for full intellectual and personal development. Crucially: learning **how** to think, not **what** to think. |
| **Logic** | The study of the methods and principles for distinguishing good arguments from bad ones. A *part* of critical thinking, not a synonym. |
| **Opinion** | A belief based solely on personal feelings rather than reason or fact. |

### 3. Perry's stages of cognitive development

From education researcher William Perry Jr., compressed by later researchers
into three levels. The arc runs from *deferring to authority* → *denying truth
exists* → *committing on evidence*.

**1 — Dualism.** Everything is right/wrong, good/bad. Knowledge is seen as
existing outside yourself, so you look to superior figures for the right answer
— professors, parents, politicians, tech billionaires. Marked by
**confirmation bias**: seeking only evidence that supports your view and
dismissing what contradicts it. Shows most clearly when confronting conflict;
such students may apply critical thinking fine in a structured classroom and
fail to outside it.

**2 — Relativism.** The overcorrection. Rather than accepting that ambiguity is
unavoidable and decisions must be made under uncertainty, students swing to the
opposite extreme: *all truth is relative, everything is just opinion.* Stating
your opinion becomes the proper mode of expression, and challenging someone
else's is looked down on. The tell-tale contradiction he points out: they still
expect the professor to back their opinion.

**3 — Commitment.** Maturity. You realise not all thinking is equally valid,
that authorities can be wrong, *and* that uncertainty is sometimes unavoidable.
From there you can still decide — committing to a position on the basis of
reason and the best available evidence.

### 4. What a good critical thinker looks like

Not one skill but a set that reinforce each other:

- **Analytical skills** — support beliefs with logic rather than personal experience or opinion; evaluate others' arguments so faulty reasoning doesn't take you in.
- **Effective communication** — writing, speaking, listening. He skips the detail, noting it was covered in last year's communications course.
- **Research and inquiry** — you cannot reach a good conclusion without going and finding the facts.
- **Flexibility and tolerance for ambiguity** — too many people take no position on a controversial issue simply because they cannot evaluate conflicting views.
- **Open-minded skepticism** — the **method of doubt**, after Descartes. Don't accept claims at face value; require them to be shown true.
- **Creative problem solving** — view problems from multiple angles, imagine scenarios in advance, and accept risk and failure as part of reaching deeper understanding.
- **Attentive, mindful, curious** — including toward your own thoughts and feelings.
- **Collaborative learning** — *not* literally studying together. Critical thinking happens in social contexts, so it means staying open to others' views, anticipating reactions, lowering your own barriers, and looking for solutions that work for both sides.

### 5. Barriers

**The three-tier model** — the book's framework for where thinking goes wrong:

| Tier | What happens | Job-rejection example |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience** | The raw event or fact | You were rejected after an interview |
| **Interpretation** | Making sense of it — often from feelings, prejudice or past experience rather than information | "I didn't get it because I lack connections" |
| **Analysis** | Raising the level: critically examining your own interpretation *and* others', refusing readings that are too narrow or too broad | Was it connections — or experience, or how you interviewed? |

Analysis is driven by **asking questions** instead of settling for the first
simple answer. His second running example is a news claim that youth voter
turnout is falling: the lazy interpretation is "young people aren't interested
in politics"; analysis asks what is actually going on and what young people
actually care about.

**Resistance** — the habitual use of immature defence mechanisms when your
worldview is challenged, because most of us dislike being proven wrong.
*(Term list is homework — see above.)*

On **struggling**, the one he demonstrates: wrestling with an issue is a
legitimate part of analysis, but some people get lost in details and what-ifs,
achieve nothing and lose time — procrastinators especially. When the struggle
becomes an end in itself, it has stopped being critical thinking.

His counter-example was a recent landslide in Nepal where a school director
evacuated roughly 900 pupils in time. He acted immediately on the facts rather
than deliberating; by his own account ten more minutes would have been fatal.

**Narrow-mindedness** — rigid beliefs that interfere with critically analysing
your own worldview. *(Term list is homework.)*
