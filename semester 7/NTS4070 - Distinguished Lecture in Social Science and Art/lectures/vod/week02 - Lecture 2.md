## The framing case: Dostoevsky

He opens by playing [a reading](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LlIEVG6GV4A) of a passage from *Crime and Punishment* — the conversation
that precedes Raskolnikov's murder of the old pawnbroker. The argument in it is
pure utilitarian arithmetic: one worthless life against thousands that her money
could save, so the crime is cancelled out by the good it enables.

**The point:** Raskolnikov decides on rational calculation alone and lets no
emotion interfere. Is that good critical thinking? The rest of the lecture is
the answer — and the answer is no. He closes the loop at the end: by the novel's
conclusion it is *emotion* — love, and guilt — that drives Raskolnikov to
confess and try to repair what he did.

## 1. What is reason?

**Definition.** Supporting a claim, conclusion or decision on the basis of
evidence. It combines the disciplined use of intelligence with the application
of rules for problem-solving.

- In **logic**, reasoning appears as arguments where a conclusion is supported by **premises**.
- In **everyday life** it is broader — it draws on creative resources and emotional discernment.
- It has a **behavioural component**: a reasonable person adjusts their behaviour to bring about the best outcome, not just concludes correctly.
- Strategies include **deduction, generalization and imagination**.
- Reasoning in a *familiar* context comes easily without training; learning the rules of logic is what lets you handle harder problems in unfamiliar ones.

**Spatial-temporal reasoning** — applying concepts of space and time — underpins
mathematics and engineering. His example is **Dr. Temple Grandin**, a professor
of animal science at Colorado State with high-functioning autism, and the
world's leading designer of livestock handling facilities. She designs by
visualising herself as the animal moving through the system, walking it and
viewing it from above, which lets her anticipate problems others miss.

## 2. Traditional views on reasoning

| View | Position |
|---|---|
| **Plato** | The soul has one rational part and two non-rational ones (emotions, physical cravings). Best state: all three in harmony, directed by the rational part. |
| **Christian thinkers** | God is the perfectly rational being; rationality is the divine spark in humans. A ranked chain of being: God → angels → humans → animals → plants. |
| **Darwin** | Rejected that anthropocentric chain. Reason **evolved** as behavioural adaptation in the struggle for survival. |
| **20th century** | Reason largely identified with science. |

He flags the irony in the last one: science rests on the assumption that a world
outside us exists — and **that assumption cannot itself be proven by reason**.
We hold foundational beliefs that reason alone can neither prove nor disprove.

## 3. Gender, race and age

Traditional Western thinking links men to mind and reason, women — via their
reproductive role — to body, nature and emotion; Plato and Aristotle were
influential here. Feminist thinkers hold that men and women share the same
rational nature, and that the gap reflects discrimination and men's reluctance
to give up their advantage at home and work.

His logical point stands independent of that dispute: **even if women were more
emotional by nature, it would not follow that they are less capable of logical
argument.** The inference doesn't go through.

The critical-thinking demand is to examine your own assumptions about sex, race
and age rather than deciding from stereotypes — unexamined assumptions distort
your perception and harm others as well as yourself. He adds that lifelong
education cultivates reasoning: more education correlates with staying mentally
productive into old age.

## 4. Dreams and problem solving

Much reasoning is not conscious. Dreams were traditionally read as a release of
suppressed emotion and irrational impulse, but current brain studies suggest
dreaming also involves activity tied to reasoning and problem-solving —
particularly **elusive visual problems**. While dreaming, the regions governing
emotion and detecting inconsistencies become more active. Mendeleev is the
famous example.

**The crucial caveat**, from Harvard psychiatrist Deirdre Barrett: creative
problem-solving in dreams happens *only after* extensive waking work on the
problem. You cannot skip the research phase and dream the answer.

> **Exercise he suggests:** keep a journal. Before sleeping, think hard about a
> problem and write it down. On waking, record the dream. Review after a while
> and see where it leads.

## 5. The role of emotion

Western culture has traditionally set emotion *against* reason, blaming it for
sloppy reasoning and bad life choices; some modern scholars dismiss it as an
evolutionary relic. He contrasts this with non-Western traditions — Native
American, Chinese, Indian, and Uzbek and Islamic — which give weight to
emotional traits such as compassion and respect.

**Emotional intelligence** — the ability to perceive, appraise and express
emotion; to generate feelings that facilitate thought; to understand emotional
knowledge; and to regulate emotion to promote intellectual growth — is
*positively* related to abstract reasoning ability.

**Positive effects:**
- **Empathy** — entering and understanding others' experience — alerts us to oppression and makes us better listeners. Empathetic people are more flexible, more open to other perspectives, and more motivated to use critical analysis.
- **Happiness and optimism** support the belief that a problem can be solved, and help people adjust to change.
- Emotions like moral indignation, love and even guilt can push us toward better decisions. In everyday life we often start from a *felt need* and only then act.

**Negative effects:**
- Emotions rooted in negative stereotypes, or anxieties from unresolved past experience — anger, fear. Fearful people give in too easily or deny a problem exists.
- We are **notoriously vulnerable to emotional appeals** in advertising and political campaigns. His examples: fear of terrorist attack; suspicion that a spouse is unfaithful with no evidence. These distract from more important issues and produce actions we regret.

**The synthesis** — the lecture's central claim: emotion and reason are a
two-branched tool. **Emotion alerts** you to problems and to other people's
perspectives and motivates you to act; **reason makes** the decision informed
and evidence-based. Governed solely by emotion you get into trouble; governed
solely by reason you get Raskolnikov.

## 7. Faith and reason

Opening questions: **can faith be achieved through reason?** and **is faith
without reason desirable?**

He notes faith is more than believing God exists — it involves trust and
obedience, and for the faithful it orients the whole of life.

### Fideism

The divine is revealed through faith and revelation, **not** reason or empirical
evidence. Held by some Christians and Muslims of a more fundamentalist bent. His
illustration is Mother Teresa, who kept her faith through periods of feeling
abandoned.

**Three weaknesses he draws out:**

1. **Conviction doesn't make something true.** Believing sincerely in Santa Claus doesn't make him real. But the reverse also holds: the absence of scientific proof of the divine doesn't establish that it doesn't exist.
2. **Which faith?** There are many visions of the divine — many religions, and many traditions and interpretations within each. Fideism gives no way to choose between them.
3. **No internal error-checking.** If faith claims are immune to reason, you cannot test them for inconsistency. How would you tell whether the 9/11 attackers acted purely on faith or on a secular political agenda? What do you do if a campus cult leader tells you to drop out or cut off your family? Blind following leaves you exposed.

### Rationalism

Religious belief should be **consistent with reason and evidence**; a claim that
conflicts with evidence is grounds for suspicion. Rationalists who accept God
argue you can reason from premises about the world to a conclusion any rational
person would accept — and attribute any conflict to a failing of science rather
than religion.

Two positions within the broader rationalist camp:
- **Richard Dawkins** — atheist; rejects faith as irrational and holds the language of faith to be meaningless, since it rests on statements that cannot be proved or disproved.
- **Agnostics** — hold God's existence ultimately unknowable. Molecular biologist **Dean Hamer** claimed in 2004 to have located a "God gene": a genetically hardwired predisposition to faith, with spirituality framed as an adaptive trait encouraging community and optimism.

### Critical rationalism

The middle position, in the tradition of *faith seeking understanding*. It
**accepts** the fideist claim that faith rests on revelation and that belief in
God is basic and needs no evidential proof — but **rejects** the claim that
faith-based assertions are immune from being disproved by reason and evidence.
There should be no logical inconsistency between revelation and reason.

His example: the great majority of Muslims reject the 9/11 attackers'
justification precisely on the grounds that such acts are **logically
inconsistent with God's goodness** — a religious argument made by reasoning.

### His conclusion

He grounds it in the historical record of atrocities committed in religion's
name — slavery in North America supported by most Christians, the medieval
Inquisition, contemporary conflicts, and scriptural justification offered for
the invasion of Iraq.

Faith disconnected from reason and evidence leaves you foundering when
challenged, and unable to evaluate competing claims of revelation — which leaves
you **vulnerable to anyone's interpretation** of what faithfulness requires. The
critical thinker's job is to balance belief and doubt, and to be willing to
question faith-based claims that conflict with evidence or reason.
