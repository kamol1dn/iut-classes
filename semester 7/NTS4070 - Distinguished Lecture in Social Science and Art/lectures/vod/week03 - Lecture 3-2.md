## Self-serving biases, continued

Part 1 covered the first of three. Here are the other two:

**2 — Taking credit for success, blaming outside forces for failure.** Students
attribute good grades to themselves — intelligence, quick grasp of material,
study skills — and poor grades to things outside their control: an unfair
teacher, being ill on exam day.

**3 — Inflating strengths, downplaying weaknesses.** We rank the traits we
happen to have (sense of humour, intelligence) as more important, and discount
the abilities we are weak in. The book's example is **physician
overconfidence** — doctors confident enough not to dig deeper into a diagnosis,
producing misdiagnoses that cost patients their lives.

## Self-fulfilling prophecy

Distorted expectations drive actions that actually bring about the expected
outcome.

He is careful to note this starts as something useful: expectations are a
**cognitive shortcut**. When they are accurate, they let you decide efficiently
instead of examining all the evidence every time. The failure mode is
oversimplification.

Two examples:

- **The Great Depression bank runs.** A few people panic and withdraw their money; the panic spreads; the collapse they feared happens *because* they feared it.
- **The student athlete.** A teacher assumes a professional footballer in the class is only there for attendance and won't study — so doesn't engage him, doesn't give him chances to speak. The student loses interest and finishes with a mediocre grade. **The teacher's own behaviour produced the result he predicted.**

## Social errors and biases

We are social creatures, and **we perceive the world differently in groups than
alone**. Groups can systematically distort both the gathering *and* the
interpretation of evidence.

### "One of us / one of them"

The brain classifies people as *ours* or *theirs* — my tribe and their tribe. We
extend respect to those similar to us and suspicion to those different by race,
gender, religion, political party, age or nationality.

He asks you to pause and think of historical or current events where this
produced catastrophe, then gives his own reading: racism, and the colonial
pattern in which powers from the global North invaded the South while telling
themselves the people there lacked certain traits and therefore needed to be
saved, educated or civilised. He notes the same structure survives today in
"we need to save the women" framings.

It also drives the **polarisation of issues into two camps** — right/left,
capitalist/socialist.

**The remedy he gives** is concrete rather than just "be aware":

1. Critically evaluate the situation.
2. **Consciously redefine who counts as "us"** on a broader basis — same dorm, same university, same country, ultimately all human beings.
3. Make a deliberate effort to stay open to perspectives you are initially certain must be wrong.

### Social expectations

Powerful enough to produce **collective delusions**, where people bend evidence
to fit their cultural worldview — and they can become institutionalised.

This closes the loop on the **Peter Reilly** case from part 1: the police held a
social expectation about what a difficult, belittling mother produces in a son,
and reasoned from that expectation to a confession rather than from evidence to
a conclusion.

**Stereotyping** is the version of this built on socially generated group labels.

### Group pressure and conformity

Group pressure can push individuals into positions they would never hold alone —
he points back to the Stanford Prison Experiment. Some religious cults exploit
it deliberately by cutting members off from dissenting family and friends. It is
strong enough to make people **deny contrary evidence directly in front of
them**.

The wish for agreement is normal. But combined with us/them sorting, it produces
exclusion of anyone who disagrees with the majority — and people gravitate
toward those who already agree with them.

**Groupthink** is the specific failure: in a meeting, someone with higher status
— authority, a better GPA, whatever the group respects — proposes an idea, and
everyone converges on it. The group is followed instead of the evidence.

His example is aimed squarely at final-year students: **marriage and wedding
pressure.** Left to your own judgement you might prefer something small and
quiet, and put the money elsewhere. But having attended everyone else's
ceremonies, an unconscious pressure builds to match the same rituals, the same
guest count, the same services. The point being that group pressure operates on
decisions you think are entirely your own.

### Diffusion of responsibility

In groups above a certain size, responsibility that is not explicitly assigned
gets treated as someone else's. **You are far more likely to help someone if you
are alone than if you are in a crowd.**

The book's case is **bystander apathy**, also called the Kitty Genovese
syndrome: in 1964 a 28-year-old woman was murdered outside her New York
apartment building over roughly half an hour, her attacker leaving twice when
residents' lights came on and returning a third time, while none of the 38
neighbours said to have heard her calls phoned the police.

> ⚠️ **Worth knowing:** the "38 witnesses who did nothing" figure comes from the
> original 1964 newspaper account and has since been substantially challenged —
> later reporting found far fewer people understood what was happening, and that
> some did contact police. The underlying effect is real and replicated; the
> famous number is not. He presents the book's version. Given how he treated the
> Stanford Prison Experiment critique in Lecture 1, flagging this is exactly the
> kind of move the course rewards.

## Closing video: Rachel Carson

He plays a short biography — deliberately the short version, noting longer ones
exist.

Carson, born 1907 in Springdale, Pennsylvania, studied zoology and genetics at
Johns Hopkins and joined the US Bureau of Fisheries, where she studied **DDT** —
an insecticide the government was promoting to farmers. She observed it killing
wildlife and called for reduced use. When the government did not act, **she
wrote a book instead**: *Silent Spring*, published 1962, which sold 65,000
copies in two weeks and caused a sensation by explaining the harm in language
ordinary people could follow.

She became the target of a smear campaign **partly financed by the chemical
industry**. She kept going — television, conferences, testimony to Congress —
and died two years after publication. Her work is widely credited as a force
behind the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency in 1970 and the
phase-out of DDT.

The thematic fit is deliberate: Carson is the counter-example to the whole
chapter. She followed the evidence against expert consensus, institutional
pressure and a funded smear campaign — and it is also the setup for the
question he then asks about defending a position that costs you something.
