## 3. The key trick: what does "worry" mean?

The whole debate turns on this word. **Speaker 1 must define it first.**

- **If we're proposition:** "worry" = *treat it as a serious risk that needs action now* (reskilling, policy, safety
  nets). It doesn't mean panic. That makes "yes" easy to defend: the other side would have to argue we should **ignore** it.
- **If we're opposition:** "worry" = *fear*, the belief that AI will mainly **destroy** jobs. We argue the evidence
  says AI **changes** jobs more than it destroys them, so the right response is **preparation and investment, not
  worry**. Never claim "AI has no impact". That claim is easy to attack. Our line is "the impact is net positive and manageable".

**The Uzbek angle, for either side.**

- **Proposition:** our growth model is cheap outsourced IT and BPO. **35% of IT Park exports are BPO/KPO**, which
  is exactly the call-centre and back-office work AI automates first (see Klarna, IBM, and the Stanford

## 5. Case outlines

### If we're PROPOSITION ("yes, we need to worry")

**Main premise:** *AI is changing work faster and deeper than any earlier technology, and the people who pay first
are young, entry-level workers, so it needs serious attention and action now.*

1. **Scale, and it's different this time (Speaker 1).** Define "worry" as taking it seriously and acting. IMF: 40% of jobs
   exposed. Goldman: 300 M job-equivalents. Earlier machines replaced **muscle**. AI replaces **thinking work**:
   writing, coding, analysis, customer service.
2. **It's already happening, to young people (Speaker 2).** Stanford "Canaries": 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs down 13%
   and more since 2022, while older workers are fine. IBM's 7,800 roles. Klarna's 700 agents. The **entry-level ladder is
   breaking**: if juniors aren't hired, who becomes the senior?
3. **Uzbekistan is exposed (Speaker 3).** 35% of IT Park exports are BPO/KPO. A young population needs jobs every year. If a
   foreign client can use AI instead of an Uzbek call centre or a junior outsourced coder, our export model weakens.
4. **Even a "net positive" needs action (Speaker 4).** WEF: 92 M people **displaced** by 2030 even if 170 M jobs are created elsewhere.
   Different people, different skills, different cities. 39% of skills change. Reskilling, education reform and safety nets
   take years. **Summary:** worry now = prepare now. Ignoring it is the real risk.

### If we're OPPOSITION ("no, we don't need to worry")

**Main premise:** *The evidence shows AI mostly transforms jobs and creates more than it destroys, so the right
response is preparation and investment, not fear.*

1. **History says no (Speaker 1).** Define "worry" as fearing mass job loss. Every wave (tractors, computers, ATMs) caused the same panic and
   ended with more jobs. Autor: 60% of today's jobs didn't exist in 1940. ATMs and tellers.
2. **The data say net creation (Speaker 2).** WEF: **+78 M net jobs** by 2030. IMF: half the exposed jobs **benefit**. ILO: most
   exposure is **augmentation**, not replacement.
3. **AI helps workers, especially weaker ones (Speaker 3).** +14% productivity, **+34% for novices** (Brynjolfsson). Klarna
   tried replacing humans and went back to hiring them. Companies need people plus AI.
4. **For Uzbekistan it's an opportunity (Speaker 4).** Lower exposure (IMF). The AI Strategy targets $1.5 bn by 2030, plus 5 million AI leaders and
   IT exports near $1 bn and growing. Worry leads to bans and protectionism. Investment leads to jobs. **Summary:** prepare, don't panic.

## 7. What they'll probably ask us (member 6 defends)

**When we're proposition**

| Likely question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "History shows tech creates jobs. Why is AI different?" | Speed and target. Past tech took decades and replaced physical tasks. AI spreads in months and hits cognitive work, and the Stanford data show the effect **already**. And even WEF's optimistic net number has 92 M people displaced. Different people get the new jobs |
| "WEF says +78 M net jobs, so why worry?" | Net is an average. It doesn't feed the 92 M who lose. And "worry" in our sense means preparing those people, which is exactly what WEF itself recommends: 39% of skills change |
| "Isn't worrying just fear-mongering?" | We defined worry as taking it seriously and acting. Fear-mongering would be calling for bans, which we don't |

**When we're opposition**

| Likely question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Young workers are already losing jobs (Stanford). Isn't that proof?" | The authors themselves call it an **early, descriptive** signal, not proof that AI caused it. It also overlaps with post-2022 interest-rate tightening that hit tech hiring. Older workers in the same jobs **grew**, so the jobs aren't disappearing, they're changing. That means better training, not worry |
| "What about call centres and BPO in Uzbekistan?" | The same AI lets Uzbek firms sell **higher-value** services (the strategy's $1.5 bn target). Klarna showed that full replacement hurts quality. The answer is upskilling those workers, which "5 million AI leaders" is designed for |
| "So you think we should do nothing?" | No. Prepare, invest, reskill. Just not *worry*, meaning fear that leads to bad policy |

## 8. Fallacy check (the midterm material is graded here too)

- Don't say "experts agree" without naming one (an **appeal to vague authority**).
- Don't use "AI will take ALL jobs" or "AI will NEVER take jobs" (**straw man** / **hasty generalisation**). The other team
  will jump on it.
- Klarna or IBM alone doesn't prove a trend (**anecdotal evidence**). Always pair an example with a statistic.
- If they attack a speaker instead of the argument, calmly point out the **ad hominem**. It earns presentation points.
